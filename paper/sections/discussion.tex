\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the results mean.} In this formulation the current LSP
assignment is genuine state, and a move constrains the future through
hold-downs, reversal costs and coupled protected-class legality. Yet a learner
that ignores the future matches a per-interval optimizer, and a learner that
plans over a 17-hour horizon does worse than both. The diagnostic explains why
there is little to plan for: once the effect of a move is felt in the interval
in which it is made, the value of a move that is predictable from the current
observation is almost entirely captured by its first-interval reward. What is
not myopic---anticipating bursts, failures and diurnal change---is not visible
in an observation without a clock or forecasts. A sequential learner pays the
price of a long-horizon objective (noisy advantages, unstable optimization)
without access to the signal that would make it worthwhile.

\paragraph{Long horizons can hide small immediate costs.} The concrete failure
is instructive for RL practice. Reconfiguration costs are small, immediate and
certain; congestion rewards are large, delayed and noisy. With $\gamma=0.995$
PPO's advantage estimates are dominated by the latter and fail to attribute
the former, so the policy settles into flipping a demand whenever the
hold-down allows. Setting $\gamma=0$ removes the oscillation and closes the
gap. The discount factor is thus a decisive hyper-parameter here, and the
default $\gamma\approx0.99$ that most RL-for-networking work inherits is not a
neutral choice when costs and benefits live on different time scales.

\paragraph{When is sequential RL justified?} Our evidence points to three
conditions, each testable before choosing an algorithm: (i) actions whose
effects are delayed relative to the decision, so that the immediate reward
does not contain them (\S\ref{sec:rq5}); (ii) exogenous dynamics that are
predictable from the observation, so that anticipation can be learned; and
(iii) decisions that change the future feasible set substantially. The
sequentiality diagnostic of \S\ref{sec:sequentiality} measures (ii) and (iii)
cheaply from a simulator, with no learning, and we recommend reporting it.

\paragraph{When should simpler methods be preferred?} When effects are
immediate, a myopic learner---or, if a model of the objective is available, a
per-interval optimizer---is both stronger and cheaper: the bandit's inference
costs \InfBanditMs{}\,ms per decision, PPO's \InfPPOMs{}\,ms and MILP-track's
\InfMilpMs{}\,ms. The bandit additionally reroutes less than half as often as
MILP-track at equal return (\TestBanditReroutes{} vs.\ \TestMilpReroutes{} per hour), % CHECK ratio at final
 which matters operationally because every LSP
move is signalling and risk.

\paragraph{Networking implications.} The results support the view of
Teal and DOTE that TE is close to one-shot, and extend it to a setting with
persistent state, incremental changes and stability timers, where one might
have expected sequential structure to matter. They also show that churn
metrics (reroutes, reversals) must be reported with the objective: two of our
PPO policies have competitive network utility but would be unacceptable to
an operator because of their oscillation.

\paragraph{Generalization.} We make no claim beyond this simulator. Larger
topologies, elastic traffic, real traffic matrices and control-plane latency
all plausibly increase temporal coupling; the diagnostic and the
delayed-effect experiment indicate how to test that.
